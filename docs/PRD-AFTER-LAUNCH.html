Which things other captains can see are earned only?

The rule every game in the research follows: never sell what signals skill; sell what signals taste. Your Flippinator is the one object the whole table watches on every flip, which makes it the highest-status thing a captain owns. League of Legends never sells its ranked-season skins and has never re-released one; Blizzard promised its skill mounts would never reach the store; Overwatch’s gold guns cost 3,000 competitive points and no money at all.

And one look, reserved. Earned things get a hand-drawn gold edge and a small idle animation; bought things get colour and pattern and never animate. Hearthstone, Destiny and Overwatch all do a version of this. Sell the gold once and gold means nothing.

Your “a coin with every sea” idea works, with one guard. Buying a sea gets you its plain coin; sailing that sea well earns its better faces. The guard: the plain coin must look plain — no gold edge, no movement — or an expensive sea starts to look like a skill. Same rule as everywhere else: the thing you can buy must never wear the thing you can only earn.

Rules every feature obeys

  • Always free to play. Money buys variety and looks, never power.
  • The rule set is gospel. No ships with powers, no house rules. New seas change the scenery and ingredients, never how sailing, docking, trading or battles work.
  • Bots and humans play by the same rules, including what they earn.
  • 13 and up. Kids may play; nothing is built for them.
  • One buyer carries the crew. Anyone at the table who owns a sea lets the whole crew sail it.
  • Money never buys dubloons, and there are no paid mystery boxes.
  • No chat with strangers. The board and a high-five, nothing typed.
  • Nothing steals attention from the voyage. No ads; nothing may slow the game.

The economy: dubloons, boxes, clout

Earned: sails with you, and shows it

  • Flippinator coin tiers. Every sea you own brings its own coin, and how far you get through that sea decides which tier you flip. The pack is bought; the tier is earned
  • Flags, won by feats
  • Pets, won by feats (the candycrab for winning through a storm). Your pet trails your boat as you sail
  • Recipes: yours once you bake one to a win, and then you can sell it in your bakery
  • Trophies: the game already awards 10 per voyage, like The Cutlass of a Thousand Notches, and accounts turn them into a cabinet
  • Rank, Daily Voyage streaks, the slipway ship
  • The cookie flag: the one bought marker, because it says “I backed the maker”, not “I’m good”. Lichess does exactly this with its patron wings

Bought or spent: at home

  • Expansion seas: money only, shared with your crew
  • Music packs: money
  • Bakery decor, ovens, pirate clothes for your pet, ship paint, sails: money or dubloons
  • Win boxes: one per voyage won, full of bakery decor, free

Dubloons

  • Earned: the dubloons left in your hold at the end of a voyage are banked. Grinding all day is allowed.
  • Spent: on looks at home. Dubloons need things to spend on, and new decor every season, or the pile grows until prices mean nothing.
  • Never given, never traded. His ruling, 15 Sep: “remove gifting doubloons between players — that way, everything in your world is stuff you either earned, or bought. It’s your world. Clean, simple, no trading.” This also ends the legal grey edge outright: with nothing transferable, no market outside the game can form.
  • Never bought, never cashed out. The terms of service ban selling dubloons or items for real money.

A new recipe every voyage

His ask: every game serves at least one recipe you don’t own yet, and says so. This is cheap, because of how the game already works: the engine deals each captain two recipes to choose between, built from a seeded handful of ingredients and then matched to the 21-recipe book. So the change is only which two it deals.

  • Player sees: one of their two cards wears a ribbon — “New to yer cookbook” — and baking it to a win puts it in the book for good.
  • The rule: if you don’t own all 21, at least one of the two is always one you don’t own.
  • Needs accounts to remember the book between voyages. Before that, it can remember in one browser only.
  • Leaves undone: what happens when a captain owns all 21 — probably a “full cookbook” mark, and then new seas bring new books.

How a player can tell earned from bought, in one glance

Six devices other games use, and what each would be here. If a captain has to open a menu to tell, the marker has failed.

  • One reserved look. A gold-leaf edge and a gentle idle movement, on earned things only. Bought things never animate. Hearthstone
  • Colour says where it came from. Rocket League’s titles are crimson for rank, green for tournaments, so the source reads instantly. Rocket League
  • A date, stamped on. “Won, Sept 2026” makes a thing unfakeable and tells a story; “rare” is just a price tag.
  • Marks that fade. Destiny’s Trials glow lasts a week, so it says right now, not once. A streak lantern beside your name that dims after a missed day does the same. Destiny 2
  • Two shelves, never mixed. “Earned at sea” and “From the chandlery” in the bakery, clearly labelled.
  • The counter shows while it fills. “7 of 10 trophies this voyage”, the way Hearthstone counts toward 500 wins under the portrait.

Two more worth stealing. Never re-award a retired thing: if a sea closes, its trophy closes with it. And mark generosity, not only winning — Destiny gives a special glow for carrying someone to their first win; here, a mark for the captain who hosted a table where a newcomer sailed their first voyage.

When the line blurs it costs real money. Diablo Immortal holds the lowest user score in Metacritic’s history after players worked out what full power cost (VGC). A skill mount merely appearing in WoW’s shop on a test server forced Blizzard to promise it never would (Game Rant). Nobody riots about selling hats.

The legal question you asked

Verdict: low risk as you ruled it, with one grey edge. This is research, not legal advice.

  • Earned boxes, with dubloons nobody can buy, are not gambling under the rules we read. Belgium: “we do not consider the game-play currency as a wager … if this cannot be purchased” (Belgium 2018). UK: items “confined for use within the game and cannot be cashed out” are “unlikely to be caught” (Gambling Commission).
  • The grey edge is trading. The Netherlands looks at the prize, not the payment: a tradable item has value because it can be sold. Every box that study tested was paid, so free boxes are untested, not cleared.
  • “Dubloons can’t be bought” isn’t the whole answer. Trading alone built real-money markets before: FIFA coins used as betting chips got two men fined in the UK in 2017 (UKGC), and CS:GO skins led Washington State to order Valve to act (Game Developer). For 13-year-olds the likelier harm is scams, and kids paying each other real money for rare decor.
  • Safeguards, strongest first: no money anywhere in the box loop (no paid keys or re-rolls); terms that ban selling dubloons, items and accounts; no outside site can move items; gift limits and waits for new accounts; the rarest prizes stay with the winner; show what a box can hold; no slot-machine drama when it opens.
  • Watch: an EU proposal on in-game currencies is planned for late 2026 (EU). Before the store opens, have a lawyer read the final box and trading design.

The ledger Medium

Player sees
Nothing directly. Their coin, flag and sea show up the same on every screen and device.
What it is
One record per captain of what they own, have earned and hold. The term is “entitlements and inventory”. The game asks it one question, e.g. “does anyone in this crew own Sushi Pirates?”, and never special-cases a captain.
The catch
A captain can edit their own browser, so the browser never writes the ledger. A trusted server writes it after checking a receipt or a finished voyage. Firebase’s security rules have to enforce that. Today the rules live only in the Firebase console, the game has no sign-in at all (a random id in the browser), and a note in stats.html records some paths as open for anyone to read and write. Rules in the repo come before any ledger.

Worlds as folders

Pasta Pirates, Planetary Pirates, Sushi Pirates, a winter or night sea. Each world is one folder: islands, ingredients, recipes, captains, ships, flags, music, art and every word it says. The game reads whichever folder is chosen and never names a world. A new world is new files, so parallel sessions never edit the same thing.

What the code looks like today (surveyed on dev, 15 Sep):

  • A world is spread over about 6 code files and 6 art folders. Ingredients and island names are in src/shared/index.js, the 21 recipes in src/ui/recipe.js and src/shared/recipe-steps.js, the words in src/shared/words.js, and art in assets/ingredients, islands, pastries, boats and sfx.
  • Captain names are typed into 9 files, including index.html. They must move to one place before a world can bring its own captains.
  • Good news: the content is already mostly tables, not logic. words.js already puts every line in one place. And the engine deals ingredients and recipes from lists, so a world folder can hand it different lists.
  • The frozen classic/ engine keeps its own copy of the source and would be left alone.

The Daily Recipe Medium

Player sees

Why this and not “the same sea”
Your instinct was right: a shared sea alone isn’t much of a hook, and a hand-designed puzzle every day is a job nobody has time for. One shared recipe turns it into a test of skill that can be compared, which is what a leaderboard needs.
Nobody designs it

Then bots play tomorrow’s sea overnight using the sea trial the repo already has: if it can’t be baked, or one seat runs away with it, the next seed is tried. Nothing reaches a player unplayed, and you never touch it.

The bake-your-recipe contest, scoped

It’s the lowest-risk kind of promotion there is, as long as it’s judged on skill, free to enter, and you never receive any food. Research, not legal advice.

  • Why it’s safe: a prize plus chance plus payment makes a lottery. Skill removes the chance and free entry removes the payment. The UK regulator says a prize competition is one where “the outcome is determined by the participants skill, judgement or knowledge” and needs no licence. Baking a real pastry clears that bar easily. Gambling Commission
  • The one thing that would break it: a random tiebreak, or picking a winner at random. That puts chance back in. Never do it.
  • Judge photos only. Nothing is posted to you, so the whole food-safety question disappears. Say plainly: bake safely, check allergies, the in-game recipes are flavour text, not cooking instructions.
  • 13 and up keeps it simple, which matches your ruling. Under-13s bring US parental-consent law; a neutral age question at entry avoids it. Under-18 winners need a parent’s email and permission. Collect only handle, email, country, the recipe and the photo. FTC UK Children’s Code
  • The photo stays theirs. You take a licence to show it on the site and your social accounts, with credit. Nothing about merchandise.
  • Platform rules: Meta requires the entrant to release Facebook and to be told the promotion is “in no way sponsored, endorsed or administered by” it, and forbids requiring shares or tagging friends. Meta The US FTC wants the hashtag itself to say it’s a contest, so use #PastryPiratesContest.

The smallest version that works: one hashtag and one form. Week 0, publish the rules and open the form. Weeks 1–3, repost entries as they come; the form does the work. Week 4, judge every entry in one evening against criteria you published before entries opened, pick five, grant their flags in the game, post the winners. Total admin: one evening, plus the rules page.

What it leaves undone: the rules page itself (15 specific lines, from the promoter’s postal address to what happens to the data), and whether prizes stay digital. Digital-only avoids shipping and tax entirely.

The order, and why

Each phase produces what the next one needs to know. Money comes after accounts, because a purchase with nowhere to live is a refund.

Phase 0 · Launch — 1 October 2026

Not this PRD: the launch bar, Cloudflare, and the Firebase budget alert.
