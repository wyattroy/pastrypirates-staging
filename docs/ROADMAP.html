What we decided, 14–17 September

Everything you ruled in this conversation, so clearing it loses nothing. The full wording lives in the repo’s decisions file.

The shape of the business

  • Success is a ladder: ready → launched → loved → earning → a studio.
  • It must pay its own Firebase bill, then $4,000 a month to you, then a helper.
  • A hobby now; a side business or company next. You’d license or sell, but not under ~$100,000.
  • Launch: 1 October 2026. Polish is the cut line.

Money

  • Always free to play. Expansions and looks are sold; power never is.
  • No ads unless nothing else works, and never during a voyage.
  • No subscriptions. No charging to play with friends.
  • One buyer carries the crew, like Jackbox.
  • Money never buys dubloons. No paid mystery boxes.
  • Seas cost money; dubloons buy looks only.

Earned vs bought

  • Never sell what signals skill; sell what signals taste.
  • Every sea brings its own coin, and its better faces are earned inside that sea.
  • Earned only: recipes, the 10 trophies the game already awards, rank, streaks, pets, win-box decor.
  • One reserved look for earned things: a gold edge and a gentle movement. Bought things never animate.
  • No trading and no gifting — your own second thought: “it’s your world. Clean, simple, no trading.”

Players and accounts

  • 13 and up. Kids may play; nothing is built for them.
  • Never sign in to play. The invitation comes at the end of a voyage.
  • An account keeps wins, recipes, trophies, looks, purchases, friends.
  • Chat only between people who know each other. Strangers get the board and a high-five. No moderation, ever.

The game itself

  • The rule set is gospel: no ships with powers, no house rules.
  • Every voyage offers a recipe you don’t own yet, and says so.
  • Your pet trails your boat and wears pirate clothes.
  • The Daily Recipe: one recipe for everyone, ranked by score, vetted overnight by bots. Puzzle ideas moved to campaign.
  • Slow voyages over days: low priority. Viewers voting, the voyage recap and Discord: back burner.

The real world

  • The wooden board is a premium edition in small numbers and the first draft of a cheaper boxed game.
  • Pitch publishers first; crowdfund only if nobody bites. The end-card sign-up is a waitlist.
  • You make the sell sheet and the video.
  • Merch stays digital first; physical only through no-stock shops, set up by a helper.
  • Cookbook next year. Contest scoped, prizes in-game.

What the research found

Seven passes, condensed. Every price here was read on the page it came from.

How comparable games make money

  • One payer brings the table — Jackbox: “only one person in your group needs to own” it; Colonist: “only 1 player needs a Membership”.
  • The base game is free; money buys variety — Ticket to Ride sells maps from $3.99 to $34.99; Wingspan sells $4.99 look-only packs beside $9.99 expansions.
  • Subscriptions suit catalogues and daily habits, not one game. Board Game Arena is 36 a year, and some members told them they treat it as a donation.
  • Donations pay bills only at scale — Lichess funds servers and one developer from millions of players.

Money for a one-person studio

  • A company that sells on your behalf (Paddle) files sales tax worldwide for 5% + 50¢ a sale. Stripe is cheaper but leaves the filing to you; its tax service starts at $90 a month. EU tax applies to a US seller.
  • Portals: Poki splits ad money 50/50 on players it sends, but wants 5 years of web exclusivity. CrazyGames has no exclusivity but you pay for multiplayer servers.
  • Stores: Steam $100 per game; Apple $99 a year and a 15% cut under $1M; Apple rejects “a repackaged website”.
  • Wordle went from under 100 players to 2 million a week in ten weeks, then sold to the NYT for low seven figures with no revenue at all. Audience is what gets priced.

Streaming and sharing

  • A game should be “a constant tutorial for anyone watching” — streamers won’t explain rules.
  • Wordle’s share grid was invented by a player and built in two weeks later; 1.2 million results were posted in the first fortnight.
  • Friends playing friends is what spreads: Among Us hit 774,000 people watching at once, two years after a flop launch.
  • Discord says over 25% of its monthly users use games inside calls.

The law, in plain terms

  • Earned boxes with a currency nobody can buy are not gambling. Belgium: a currency doesn’t count as a wager “if this cannot be purchased”. UK: items that can’t be cashed out are “unlikely to be caught”.
  • Trading was the risk, and you removed it. The Dutch test treats an item as valuable the moment it can be transferred. FIFA coins and CS:GO skins both grew real-money markets that regulators moved against.
  • Paid boxes would have needed published odds (Apple and Google), and the US FTC now requires a parent’s consent under 16. You ruled them out entirely.
  • Kids: 13-and-up avoids US parental-consent law for sign-ins and purchases.

The board game

  • Kickstarter tabletop campaigns funded 80% of the time in 2024; twelve first-time creators averaged about $39,000 from 782 backers. Shipping is where campaigns lose money.
  • Factories won’t print under ~500 copies; one designer’s 1,250-copy run landed at about $8.50 a copy.
  • Royalties for new designers run about 5–8%; Stonemaier pays a $10,000 advance but won’t consider a game that tops out at four players, and avoids games already published.
  • Your four-seat rule closes two doors (Stonemaier, Calliope) before anyone reads a rule. A design question, and yours.
  • Nobody signs confidentiality agreements; never mail a prototype nobody asked for; agents barely exist.

Merch and food

  • Print-on-demand costs nothing up front: Printful tees start at $9.25–$13.50 base; Redbubble pays artists 20%. Makeship makes a plush only if 200 fans preorder in 21 days.
  • The Official Stardew Valley Cookbook became a New York Times bestseller. Catan and Ticket to Ride have official cookbooks. No documented bakery partnership with an indie game turned up — open water.

What could stop 1 October

  1. The three jobs nothing automatic can check. This is the sharpest thing anyone said today, and it came from Dev. Overnight, a fix it made for one of your bugs quietly broke a purchase’s coins on staging — every automated check stayed green the whole hour, and it was caught only because Wy-Blade ran a phone pose nobody had asked for. The lesson for this plan: the polish you agreed to cut is the part that has automatic coverage. The cutover, the database rules and the private repository have none. All three are done by hand, once, on the live domain — and unlike a bad merge, none can be undone by reverting anything. So every spare hour between now and the 26th goes to checking those three, not to the game.
  2. The domain. Moving nameservers with the lock still live takes the site down for real players. Handled by doing your five-minute job a day early, and by not touching the live domain until staging has proved itself.
  3. The bill. The database answers strangers today, and you’re on a pay-as-you-go plan. Traffic you’re hoping for is also the traffic that bills you. The alert and the scoped rules both land before launch.
  4. 161 commits arriving at once. Everything since 13 September reaches players in a single merge, so the whole-game check runs on the final build, not on pieces.
  5. Polish appetite. The known-bugs list is long and none of it is game-stopping. Your cut line is what protects the date; I’ll hold it unless you say otherwise.
  6. Nobody comes. A launch post is one day of attention. The share card and the Daily Recipe are what make day eight look like day one, which is why they lead the after-launch work.
